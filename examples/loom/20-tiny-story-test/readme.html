Lesson 20 — a tiny complete story

Everything you've learned, in one fifty-line story. Open lesson.loom alongside this page and trace the moves you recognise. There's no new syntax here — just every old syntax in one place.

What you'll see

  • Lesson 1–4 — a document header with a title and :conversation tag, three declared cast members with labels.
  • Lesson 5 — five named sections that fall through or divert.
  • Lesson 6 — flavor lines setting the scene at every beat.
  • Lesson 7–8 — choices with mixed once-only and sticky markers, plus diverts to named sections.
  • Lesson 9–10 — three variables and interpolation in dialogue.
  • Lesson 11–12 — a let binding gating a choice via if.
  • Lesson 13–14 — ledger queries (played, chose) and a fired event read back with since.
  • Lesson 15 — an after / otherwise pair that varies a beat by the player's choice.
  • Lesson 16 — an each visit block at the hub that evolves over repeated returns.
  • Lesson 17 — a match on a string variable that branches three ways.
  • Lesson 18 — inline assigns inside a single dialogue line.
  • Lesson 19 — a backlink in narration prose.

The story

Wren and Alex meet at the harbor of Saltmere. The bell hasn't rung in three days. The keeper, Maren, is missing. The player picks where to search and what to ask, and Wren's trust in them rises or falls accordingly. The story ends one of three ways depending on what they found and how they treated her.

Reading it

Start by skimming the file end to end. Notice that it still reads like a screenplay — strip the brackets in your head and the structure is recognisable: scene heading, action, character, dialogue.

Then start at the top and play it through mentally. When you hit a branch, follow each side. When you hit a guard, ask what makes it true. When you hit each visit, picture the player returning twice.

This is what a real Loom document looks like when no single feature is doing all the work — every tool gets used a little, and the story still fits on one page.

Where to go from here

  • Read docs/dev/loom-design.md for the full design treatment — characters with dispositions, factions, the live- performance layer, generators, the scheduler.
  • Read docs/dev/loom-grammar.md for the formal grammar — every production, every operator.
  • Write your own. Start with a single section, one cast member, one choice. Grow it.
The whole point. Loom is for writers first. If at any point in the tour something felt like programming-for-its-own-sake, ignore that part — the language only really earns its keep when the story it expresses is one you couldn't write any other way.
